[s10-p000] Expectation & Variance
[s10-p001] •  Expectation E[X]: the long-run average value of X.
[s10-p002] •  E[X] = Σₓ x P(X=x)  (discrete)      E[X] = ∫ x fX(x) dx         (continuous)
[s10-p003] • Variance Var(X): how spread out X is around its mean.
[s10-p004] •  Var(X) = E[(X - E[X])²]
[s10-p005] • Standard deviation = √Var(X); larger σ means more fluctuation.
[s10-p006] 	σ = 1 (low spread)              σ = 2 (high spread)
[s10-p007] Both curves are centered at μ = 0, but the wider curve
[s10-p008] represents a more uncertain / noisier variable.

[s13-p000] Worked Example: Conditional Probability
[s13-p001] •  Problem: Roll a fair die. Let A = “result is even”, B = “result is ≥ 4” . Find P(A|B).
[s13-p002] •  Step 1: List outcomes.  A = {2,4,6},  B = {4,5,6}
[s13-p003] •  Step 2: Find A∩B = {4,6}, so P(A∩B) = 2/6
[s13-p004] •  Step 3: P(B) = 3/6
[s13-p005] •  Step 4: P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) = (2/6)/(3/6) = 2/3
[s13-p006] •  Interpretation: once we know the roll is ≥ 4, the chance it is also even rises from 1/2 to 2/3.
[s13-p007] PART  I  -  FOUNDATIONS

[s14-p001] •  P(A) = Σᵢ P(A|Bᵢ) P(Bᵢ)

[s16-p000] • P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) / P(B)
[s16-p001] •  P(A): prior -- belief before seeing evidence
[s16-p002] •  P(B|A): likelihood -- probability of the evidence if A is true
[s16-p003] •  P(A|B): posterior -- updated belief after seeing evidence
[s16-p004] •  P(B): evidence -- overall probability of the         observation
[s16-p005] • posterior α prior × likelihood
[s16-p006] Used in: naive Bayes classification, medical diagnosis       updating, spam filtering from words, information
[s16-p007] retrieval.
[s16-p008] P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B)
[s16-p009] Derived directly from the product rule P(A∩B) =
[s16-p010] P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A).

[s18-p000] Worked Example: Medical Diagnosis (Bayes)
[s18-p001] •  Problem: Disease prevalence P(disease) = 0.01. Test sensitivity P(+|disease) = 0.90. False-positive rate P(+|no disease) = 0.05. Given a POSITIVE test, find P(disease|+).
[s18-p002] •  Step 1 - Prior: P(disease) = 0.01, P(no disease) = 0.99
[s18-p003] •  Step 2 - Likelihoods: P(+|disease) = 0.90,  P(+|no disease) = 0.05
[s18-p004] •  Step 3 - Evidence: P(+) = 0.90(0.01) + 0.05(0.99) = 0.0585
[s18-p005] •  Step 4 - Posterior: P(disease|+) = 0.90(0.01) / 0.0585 ≈ 15.4%
[s18-p006] •  Interpretation: a positive result does NOT imply high disease probability, because the prior prevalence is low and false positives are common relative to true cases.

[s19-p009] Common Misconception
[s19-p010] “Positive test = 90% probability of disease.” This        confuses P(+|disease) = 0.90 (the sensitivity)
[s19-p011] with P(disease|+) (the posterior).
[s19-p012] Among the 585 people who test positive, only 90      actually have the disease -- the other 495 are
[s19-p013] false alarms from the huge healthy population.

[s23-p000] Information Theory: Entropy
[s23-p001] •  Entropy H(X) = -Σₓ P(X=x) log P(X=x)
[s23-p002] •  Entropy measures uncertainty: higher H(X) =        more uncertain outcome.
[s23-p003] •  Binary example: entropy is highest at p=0.5
[s23-p004] (maximum uncertainty), and lowest near p=0 or   p=1 (nearly certain).
[s23-p005] •  Maximum-entropy principle: choose the least-      committal distribution consistent with what you   know.
[s23-p006] •  Loosely connects to logistic regression and to                  Bayesian reasoning without overcommitting.

[s26-p002] • What it models: one binary trial

[s41-p000] •  Idea: choose the parameter value that makes the observed data MOST LIKELY.
[s41-p001] •  θ̂_MLE = argmax_θ p(D|θ)
[s41-p002] •  For i.i.d. data: L(θ;D) = ∏ᵢ p(xᵢ|θ)

[s41-p004] •  log L = Σᵢ log p(xᵢ|θ)
[s41-p005] •  Why log? Turns a product into a sum -- easier to   differentiate and numerically stable.

[s42-p000] Worked Example: Bernoulli MLE
[s42-p001] •  Problem: 100 users visit a page; 70 click. Estimate the click probability p.
[s42-p002] Step 1: L(p) = pN1  (1-p)N0,  with N1=70 (successes), N0=30 (failures)
[s42-p003] •  Step 2: log-likelihood ℓ(p) = N1 log p + N0 log(1-p)
[s42-p004] •  Step 3: Set derivative to zero: N1/p - N0/(1-p) = 0
[s42-p005] •  Step 4: Solve → p̂ = N1/n = 70/100 = 0.70
[s42-p006] •  Interpretation: the MLE is simply the observed success fraction. This connects directly to cross-entropy training in neural nets -- minimizing cross-entropy is maximizing likelihood.
[s42-p007] PART  III  -  M LE

[s69-p000] Common Misconceptions
[s69-p001] •  1. Probability density ≠ probability (a point always has probability 0 for continuous X).
[s69-p002] •  2. P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A) -- conditioning direction matters.
[s69-p003] •  3. A positive test does not imply a high posterior probability of disease.
[s69-p004] •  4. CLT does NOT mean the original data are Gaussian -- it's about standardized sample averages.
[s69-p005] •  5. Softmax confidence is not automatically a calibrated probability.
[s69-p006] •  6. Independence is a modeling assumption, not a universal fact.
[s69-p007] •  7. MLE gives a point estimate, not the complete uncertainty picture.
[s69-p008] •  8. A familiar distribution (e.g. Gaussian) isn't necessarily the correct model.

[s72-p001] Gaussian MLE: Full Derivation
[s72-p002] •  L(μ,σ) 〓 Πᵢ (1/(σ√ (2π))) exp(-(xᵢ-μ)2/(2σ2))
[s72-p003] •  ℓ(μ,σ) 〓 -(n/2)log(2π) - n log σ - (1/(2σ2)) Σᵢ(xᵢ-μ)2
[s72-p004] •   ∂ℓ/∂μ 〓 0  →  μ^ 〓 -x 〓 (1/n) Σᵢ xᵢ
[s72-p005] •  ∂ℓ/∂σ2 〓 0  →  σ^2 〓 (1/n) Σᵢ (xᵢ - -x)2
[s72-p006] •  Note: this MLE variance divides by n, and is biased downward relative to the unbiased n-1 denominator.
[s73-p000] Bernoulli, Exponential, Poisson MLE Derivations
[s73-p001] Bernoulli: L(p) = pN1  (1-p)N0,  ℓ(p) = N1 log p + N0 log(1-p)
[s73-p002] •  Score equation: N1/p - N0/(1-p) = 0  →  p̂ = N1/n
[s73-p003] •  Exponential: L(λ) = λ ⁿ exp(-λΣᵢxᵢ)  →  λ̂ = 1/x̄
[s73-p004] Poisson: L(λ) = ∏ᵢ λxᵢ  e−λ / xᵢ!  →  λ̂ = (1/n)Σᵢ xᵢ = x̄
[s73-p005] •  All three MLEs reduce to a simple, familiar descriptive statistic (a fraction or a sample mean).
[s73-p006] APPENDIX
[s74-p000] Worked Example: Exponential MLE (Service Waiting Times)
[s74-p001] •  Problem: 30 service waiting times, sample mean x̄ = 1.283 hours. Fit an exponential model.
[s74-p002] •  Step 1: For exponential data, λ̂ = 1/x̄
[s74-p003] •  Step 2: λ̂ = 1/1.283 ≈ 0.779 per hour
[s74-p004] •  Step 3: Fisher information I(λ) = n/λ²  →  SE(λ̂) ≈ λ̂/√ n = 0.779/√30 ≈ 0.142
[s74-p005] •  Step 4: Approximate 95% interval: [0.500, 1.058]
[s74-p006] •  Interpretation: the estimate carries a quantified uncertainty band, not just a point value.

[s75-p000] Fisher Information & Asymptotic Normality
[s75-p001] • Observed Fisher information: |(θ̂) = -ℓ丨丨(θ̂)
[s75-p002] • Approximate variance: Var(θ̂) ≈ |(θ̂)_1
[s75-p003] • Under regularity conditions, MLE is consistent and asymptotically normal:
[s75-p004] • √ n (θ̂ - θ*) ⇒ Normal(0, l(θ*)_1)
[s75-p005] • MLE can attain the Cramér-Rao lower bound.
[s75-p006] • Small samples: bias and large variance are possible; boundary cases (e.g. all-success Bernoulli) give p̂=1 with no automatic uncertainty measure.

[s77-p000] Extra Bayesian Formulas: Conjugate Updates
[s77-p001] •  Beta-Bernoulli: Beta(α,β) + N1 successes, N0 failures  →  Beta(α+N1, β+N0)
[s77-p002] •  Dirichlet-Multinomial: Dirichlet(α) + category counts k  →  Dirichlet(α + k)
